# Facilitation Tips for Strategic Sessions

Strategic sessions fail not because the tools are wrong but because the room dynamics undermine them. Ideas die too early, convergence takes too long, the most senior voice crowds out the best answer, and the team leaves without momentum. These five practical tips address that execution layer — the physical, social, and process choices that determine whether a Rollercoaster session produces genuine insight or polite consensus.

---

## Tip 1: The Third Solution — Keep Searching

### The problem

After finding a first option, the internal voice offers praise and the search stops. This is the **Premature Satisfaction (Coach A Internal Dialogue)** anti-pattern: *"Well done, you're a star, call it a day"* — and the best option is left undiscovered.

### The principle

> On complex strategic problems, one should deliberately seek a third solution, because early options are rarely the best and the process of finding a third option increases the odds of discovering an even better fourth. _source: ch20 §Tip 1_

The book names three internal-dialogue archetypes drawn from NLP:

| Coach | Style | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Coach A** | Pure praise | Stops search out of satisfaction |
| **Coach B** (optimal) | Credits effort + maintains tension | Keeps the search alive |
| **Coach C** | Harsh criticism | Stops search out of dejection |

The **Coach C** mirror image — **Dejection / Self-Critical Block** — is equally dangerous: *"That's probably not the answer, it's too hard for you"* causes abandonment after the first failed attempt.

### Rule 1: Ask the right question

> When facing a complex strategic problem, ask yourself: 'What are two widely different solutions to this problem?' — not 'Are there two solutions?' — to frame the problem as solvable rather than open-ended. _source: ch20 §Tip 1_

### Workflow: Find the Third (and Best) Solution

**Trigger:** You have generated one or two initial options and feel the urge to stop.

1. Ask: *"What are two widely different solutions to this problem?"* — treat it as a riddle, not a black hole.
2. Celebrate finding option one without claiming victory — note it as "egg one."
3. Continue searching for a second, widely different option.
4. Tell yourself: *"The third solution is often the best"* — use this to re-energise the search.
5. Generate a third option, which may be a synthesis or middle ground of the first two.
6. Recognise that having three options means a fourth is likely nearby — keep searching a little longer.
7. Once three to four options exist, evaluate them; the third or fourth is statistically most likely to be the strongest.

## Tip 2: Calibrate Team Size to Phase

### The principle

> Small teams should be used for the **Up** (divergent/generative) phase of strategic problem-solving, and large teams for the **Down** (evaluative/critical) phase. _source: ch20 §Tip 2_

Small teams generate ideas faster because every participant's contribution is noticed. Large teams are better at rapidly spotting flaws because more expertise is present to challenge ideas.

### Problem-Solving Temperaments

The book introduces three archetypes from A. A. Milne's characters:

- **Tigger** — thrives in divergent Up phases and the final Push; impulsive, energetic, idea-generating.
- **Eeyore** — cautious; excels at spotting flaws in the Down phase.
- **Pooh** — balanced team player; contributes across all phases, even-keeled facilitator.

**Rule 2:** Any strategic taskforce should aim for at least a third Tiggers, and preferably a third each of Tiggers, Eeyores, and Poohs.

### Decision rule: small vs. large team

| Situation | Team size |
|---|---|
| Goal is idea generation (Up phase) | **4–5 people** per sub-group |
| Goal is critical evaluation and flaw-spotting (Down phase) | **12–16 people** in full plenary |

Do not use a large plenary group for Up sessions — someone will shoot down germinal ideas before they develop. Do not use a small team alone for Down sessions — you will miss the breadth of expertise needed to spot all flaws.

### The Two-Meeting Process

**Trigger:** A complex strategic issue needs to be tackled by a cross-functional team in an established organisation.

**Rule 3 — Memo before the first meeting:** Circulate a memo describing the problem to all participants before the first meeting: not too much data, half a page maximum.

**Meeting 1 (Up session — ~1 hour):**
- Split 12–16 participants into three or four small teams of four to five people, randomly mixing functions and seniority.
- Ensure at least one Tigger per team.
- Teams work in parallel on the same Up technique; teams swap after 15–20 minutes.
- Each participant votes on their three favourite ideas (excluding their own).

**Rule 4 — Memo after the Up session:** Circulate a memo capturing the five to ten best ideas: a few lines per idea, a catchy name, and a sense of the upside.

**Meeting 2 (Down session — ~1 hour):**
- Run as a full plenary session.
- Surface all objections and act upon them immediately.

**Rule 5 — Sub-groups after the Down session:** Task three specific sub-groups with progressing the final three best ideas, one idea per sub-group.

## Tip 3: Post-its and Standing Positions

### The problem

Capturing ideas on paper or whiteboard creates a psychological barrier to revision. Facilitators resist marginal but valid suggestions because incorporating them means *"redrawing the whole thing."* This is the **High-Revision-Cost Physical Support** anti-pattern.

### The principle

> Post-its used while standing are the superior physical medium for capturing strategic work-in-progress, because they minimise the cost of revision and therefore lower the threshold for accepting valuable team suggestions. _source: ch20 §Tip 3_

**Rule 6:** Use Post-its on a wall or window — not paper or whiteboard — to capture work-in-progress during strategic exercises.

**Rule 7:** Get participants to stand up for Up (generative) strategic exercises.

Seated, laptop-centric, heads-down work during a supposed idea-generation meeting is the **Sitting Down for Creative/Strategic Work** anti-pattern. Standing activates both right-brain intuitive and left-brain structured idea generation.

## Tip 4: Vote First, Then Debate

### The two failure modes of convergence

| Anti-pattern | Description | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **HiPPO Convergence** | The Highest Paid Person's Opinion chooses in 10 seconds; others assent without voicing disagreement. | Silent dots voting before any debate — every view is registered first. |
| **Endless Collaborative Debate** | Participants with tiny differences of opinion debate at length, consuming disproportionate time on low-stakes distinctions. | Vote first; open debate only where disagreement is material and outcomes differ. |
| **Bike-shedding** | Groups devote extensive time to trivial familiar matters (a bike shed) while rubber-stamping complex unfamiliar decisions (a nuclear power station). | Name the relative importance of the issue explicitly; use dots to expose whether the group actually disagrees on the important matters. |

### The principle

> In convergent thinking, teams should vote first and then debate — not the other way around. _source: ch20 §Tip 4_

Voting first surfaces the distribution of views instantly, focuses debate only on the issues where disagreement is material, and prevents time being wasted on matters the group broadly agrees on.

### Dots Voting Workflow

**Trigger:** A team has finished the divergent phase and needs to converge on priorities.

1. Distribute a batch of colourful sticky dots to each participant.
2. Instruct participants to place their dots on their preferred options — **in silence, within 10 seconds**.
3. Observe the distribution of dots to identify the majority view and any significant dissent.
4. Open debate **only** for issues where (a) disagreement is material and (b) the outcome differs meaningfully between options.
5. Avoid debating items where broad consensus is visible or where the outcome is the same under either option.
6. **Rule 8:** If two options produce identical outcomes, pick one — use a coin flip if needed.

### Decision rule: is this disagreement worth debating?

**Debate when:**
- The issue is strategically important.
- The two options produce materially different outcomes.
- After voting, a significant minority still disagrees with the majority view.

**Do not debate when:**
- The issue is low-stakes or bike-shedding territory.
- The two options produce the same outcome.
- The dots reveal broad consensus — the disagreement is a tiny minority.

## Tip 5: AI's Role in the Rollercoaster

### The principle

> Strategic thinkers should remain aware of AI's evolving capabilities and limitations, particularly its use in Down and Push phases, without over-estimating its current capacity for creative Up phase thinking. _source: ch20 §Tip 5_

Three key limitations the book names:

1. **Dataset bias from the past** — AI works probabilistically on historical data.
2. **Values skew from programmers** — outputs reflect the biases of those who built the system.
3. **Unclear accountability** — responsibility for AI-generated strategic advice is unresolved.

### Decision rule: which phase can AI support today?

| Phase | AI fit | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Up** (creative generation) | ✗ Not yet capable | AI remains closer to *"a giant spelling auto-correct"* than a creative partner. |
| **Down** (filtering/prioritising) | ✓ Suited | Can help reduce thousands of Mutation Game options to a manageable shortlist. |
| **Push** (social proof) | ✓ Suited | Can sieve millions of social media posts to assess early Lean test reception of a new idea. |

## When to Use / When NOT to Use

**Apply these tips when:**
- Running any Up phase workshop (Pyramid Principle, Happy Line, Mutation Game) — use small teams, Post-its, standing positions, and the Third Solution discipline.
- Running any Down phase session (Payoff Profiles, Landscape Analysis, Lean Startup) — use full plenary, Eeyore temperaments, and dots voting for convergence.
- Any session where convergence is needed — vote before you debate, without exception.
- Preparing a two-meeting process — issue memos before and after the Up session.

**Do not apply rigidly when:**
- The problem is not truly strategic (i.e., it has a known solution, requires no trade-offs, and has no uncertainty). For solo analytical work with a clear answer, the Third Solution principle and team-sizing rules add friction without benefit. See [The Strategic Mindset](../core/strategic-mindset.md) for the six conditions that make a problem truly strategic.
- The team is a startup of four to six people — the 12–16 taskforce rule does not apply; the principle of small-for-Up, large-for-Down still holds proportionally.

## Code Examples

The following excerpt illustrates the **dots voting convergence** workflow and the **vote-before-debate rule** as they apply across all convergent moments in the Rollercoaster — Happy Line ranking, Mutation Game shortlisting, Payoff Profiles prioritisation:

```
DOTS VOTING PROTOCOL (applies at any Up→Down transition)

Before opening debate:
  1. Distribute colourful sticky dots — one batch per participant.
  2. Silent vote: each participant places dots on preferred Post-its.
     Time limit: 10 seconds.
  3. Read the dot distribution.

Then:
  IF broad consensus visible (dots clustered on 1–2 items):
    → Skip debate on those items. They are decided.
  IF significant minority dissent (dots split materially):
    → Open debate ONLY on those items.
  IF two options produce the same outcome:
    → Pick one. Coin flip is acceptable. Do not debate.
```

The two-meeting memo protocol:

```
PRE-MEETING MEMO (circulate before Meeting 1 — Up session)
  - Author: issue owner
  - Length: half a page MAXIMUM
  - Content: problem description only; not too much data
  - Purpose: prime participants without priming their answers

POST-UP MEMO (circulate between Meeting 1 and Meeting 2)
  - Author: issue owner
  - Content: five to ten best ideas from the Up session
  - Format per idea:
      • Catchy name
      • A few lines of description
      • Sense of the upside
  - Purpose: allow Eeyores to prepare critiques for the Down session
```

The Third Solution internal prompt:

```
INTERNAL DIALOGUE TEMPLATE (Coach B stance)

After finding option one:
  "Good — that's egg one. What are two WIDELY DIFFERENT
   solutions to this problem?"

After finding option two:
  "The third solution is often the best. Keep going."

After finding option three:
  "Three means four is nearby. One more search before evaluating."

Evaluation gate: only assess options once three or four exist.
```
