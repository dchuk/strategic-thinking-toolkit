# Compelling Story

A strategic project that ends with a technically correct but unconvincing presentation has failed. The analysis is done, the data is in, the recommendation is clear — yet the audience walks away uncertain, uncommitted, or simply unable to remember what you said. The Compelling Story toolkit solves this: it combines the Pyramid Principle applied with **Conviction** (not just Clarity) with three **Adland Swagger** techniques borrowed from advertising to make the recommendation both rigorous and memorable.

---

## The Shift from Clarity to Conviction

Early in a project, the Pyramid Principle creates **Clarity** — it organises hypotheses and structures what you need to find out. At project end, it delivers **Conviction** — the hypotheses have been replaced by facts, and the story is told assertively from the top down.

The key language shift:

| Project phase | Language mode | Rhetorical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Early (Clarity) | 'If A then B' | Credible — opens possibility |
| End (Conviction) | 'Because A, therefore B' | Compelling — asserts conclusion |

> **Principle:** 'Because' and 'therefore' make a story compelling; 'if' and 'then' only make it credible. _source: ch18 §Examples of Pyramids used throughout projects_

> **Principle:** Following Minto's rules early in a project is useful; following them at the end is essential for your story to prove compelling. _source: ch18 §In summary_

The pyramid structure itself does not change when facts arrive — only the words inside each Post-it change. The logic scaffold is reusable.

---

## Minto's Five Rules for the End-of-Project Pyramid

Apply these in sequence when building your final presentation.

### Rule 1: Structure your ideas in a pyramid form

One Post-it at the apex stating the conclusion. Two or three supporting Post-its beneath it. Continue downward, narrowing to data at the bottom.

### Rule 2: Present your ideas from the top down

State the conclusion first, then support it with details. At the point of Conviction you know the answer, so you can be crisp and assertive rather than building suspense.

### Rule 3: Ideas in the pyramid have to obey three sub-rules

**Sub-rule 1 — Summaries:** Ideas at each level must be summaries of the ideas grouped below them.

**Sub-rule 2 — Inductive or deductive groupings:** Every horizontal grouping must use either a pure **deductive** or a pure **inductive** argument. No mixed logic within a single grouping.

- **Deductive argument:** Because A and B, therefore C. The conclusion is derived logically from the supporting facts. Use when you have facts that allow logical derivation.
- **Inductive argument:** A parallel set of examples from which a general conclusion can be induced. The logic feels looser. Use when deductive logic is not available.

**Sub-rule 3 — Logical order:** Place the most important Post-it on the left within a grouping. Order the rest to tell the story most effectively for the specific audience.

### Rule 4: Make sure the vertical relationships work

Test every parent–child connection:
- Moving **down**: ask 'Why?' — the child Post-its answer 'because / and'.
- Moving **up**: the child Post-its produce 'therefore' at the parent level.

### Rule 5: Make sure the horizontal relationships work

- **Deductive grouping:** The second Post-it must comment on (refer back to) the first Post-it.
- **Inductive grouping:** All Post-its in the grouping must be summarisable as a single plural statement.

## Adland Swagger

Once the pyramid is structurally sound, augment it with three persuasion techniques borrowed from advertising.

> **Principle:** The Adland Swagger formula for a compelling story is: **1 visual, 2 people, 3 words**. _source: ch18 §IN SUMMARY (Adland Swagger)_

### Three-Word Summary

Distil the entire recommendation into three (or four) words. A three-word summary is highly portable: colleagues can spread the word without access to the full business case. It addresses auditory and kinaesthetic communication preferences.

### One Simple Visual

Select or create a single, crisp image that captures the core concept at a glance — a framework diagram, a striking photograph, a single chart. Over a third of people have a strong visual preference; one clear image extends reach beyond those who read the full deck.

### Two Human Anecdotes

Include exactly two representative individual stories — from customers, employees, or competitors — to give a human face to the analysis.

- **One** anecdote is too risky: if that individual turns out to be unrepresentative, the entire qualitative case is invalidated.
- **Three or more** anecdotes introduce too much variety in views, diluting and fragmenting the core message.
- **Two** is the formula: one is too risky, two is the charm.

## Workflows

### Workflow A: Build an End-of-Project Pyramid

**Trigger:** You have finished data gathering and need to present a final recommendation.

1. State the top-level conclusion assertively as a single Post-it at the apex — no 'if' language.
2. Structure the presentation in pyramid form: two or three supporting Post-its beneath each Post-it, continuing downward.
3. Check sub-rule 1: confirm that each Post-it is a true summary of the Post-its grouped below it.
4. Check sub-rule 2: confirm that each horizontal grouping uses either a pure deductive or a pure inductive argument (not mixed).
5. Check sub-rule 3: order Post-its within each grouping from most important (left) to least, tailored to the specific audience.
6. Verify vertical relationships: read up ('therefore') and down ('why / because / and') through every parent–child pair.
7. Verify horizontal relationships: in deductive groupings confirm the second Post-it comments on the first; in inductive groupings confirm all Post-its can be summarised as one plural statement.
8. Update any Post-its whose wording no longer matches the facts gathered, then cascade the change upward through the pyramid.

_source: ch18 §MINTO'S RULES FOR USING THE PYRAMID PRINCIPLE AT THE END OF PROJECTS_

### Workflow B: Package a Recommendation with Adland Swagger

**Trigger:** You have a pyramid-structured recommendation and need to make it maximally persuasive for an audience.

1. Distil the entire recommendation into a three- (or four-) word summary that colleagues can repeat without the full deck.
2. Create or select one simple visual that captures the core concept at a glance.
3. Identify two representative individuals (customers, employees, or competitors) whose stories humanise the analysis.
4. Weave the three-word summary, one visual, and two anecdotes into your presentation alongside the pyramid structure.
5. Optionally explore additional advertising techniques (music, colour, repetition) from practitioners such as Ogilvy, Hegarty, or Godin.

_source: ch18 §Adland Swagger and §IN SUMMARY_

## When to Use / When NOT to Use

**Use Compelling Story when:**
- You are at the end of a strategic project presenting a final recommendation (Conviction phase).
- You have real data replacing earlier hypotheses — the shift from 'if' to 'because/therefore' is only honest when facts exist.
- Your audience needs to act on, fund, or champion the recommendation.
- The recommendation needs to travel beyond the room — the Three-Word Summary lets it spread without the full deck.

**Do NOT use (or adapt) when:**
- You are early in a project and still structuring hypotheses — use the Pyramid Principle for Clarity instead (see [pyramid-principle.md](pyramid-principle.md)).
- You have no data yet: assertive top-down framing without underlying facts is misleading, not compelling.
- The audience only needs a status update, not a recommendation — a simpler communication format suffices.
- You cannot identify two truly representative anecdotes: do not force the Adland Swagger formula if the human stories would misrepresent the data.

## Anti-Patterns

### Using 'if' language at the end of a project

Retaining hypothetical 'if … then' framing when facts are already available signals a lack of conviction and keeps the story in the credibility zone rather than the compelling zone.

**Fix:** Replace all 'if' Post-its with assertive, fact-based statements. Shift language from 'if A then B' to 'because A, therefore B'.

**Signals:** Presentation contains the word 'if' before key claims; conclusions are framed as conditional rather than declarative; audience is left uncertain whether the team actually has data. _source: ch18 opening paragraphs_

---

### Relying on a single human anecdote

One individual's story risks invalidating the case if that individual turns out to be unrepresentative.

**Fix:** Select two representative individuals whose stories together give a human face to the analysis without introducing contradictory variety. _source: ch18 §TWO HUMAN ANECDOTES_

---

### Using three or more human anecdotes

Three or more individual stories introduce too much variety in views, diluting and fragmenting the core message.

**Fix:** Trim to exactly two representative anecdotes that reinforce the same strategic direction. _source: ch18 §TWO HUMAN ANECDOTES_

## Code Examples

The following schematic illustrates a correct end-of-project pyramid with vertical and horizontal relationship checks.

```
APEX (top Post-it — assertive conclusion)
  └── "Because [Branch A] and [Branch B], therefore [Apex]"

BRANCH A (summary Post-it)                   BRANCH B (summary Post-it)
  └── Deductive grouping:                      └── Inductive grouping:
      Post-it A1 (fact)                            Post-it B1 (example)
      Post-it A2 (comments on A1)                  Post-it B2 (example)
      → A2 comments on A1 ✓                        Post-it B3 (example)
      → A1 + A2 → Branch A ✓                       → B1, B2, B3 = plural ✓

VERTICAL CHECK (any branch):
  Down: "Why is Branch A true?" → "Because A1 and A2"
  Up:   "A1 and A2, therefore Branch A" ✓

HORIZONTAL CHECK — deductive (Branch A):
  "Does A2 comment on A1?" → Yes ✓

HORIZONTAL CHECK — inductive (Branch B):
  "Can B1 + B2 + B3 be summarised as one plural statement?" → Yes ✓
```

```
ADLAND SWAGGER CHECKLIST

[ ] Three-word summary written: ____  ____  ____
[ ] One visual selected: __________________________
[ ] Anecdote 1 (representative individual): ________
[ ] Anecdote 2 (representative individual): ________
[ ] All three woven into the presentation deck

Formula: 1 visual + 2 people + 3 words
```

```
LANGUAGE AUDIT — run before final presentation

Replace every instance of:              With:
"If we do X, then Y will happen"   →   "Because we did X, Y has happened"
"If the data shows A…"             →   "The data shows A; therefore…"
"This could suggest…"              →   "This confirms…"
"We believe that if…"              →   "The evidence shows that…"
```
