# Pyramid Principle

You have a complex qualitative question — "Should we enter this market?", "How can we be more successful at customer retention?" — and a deadline. Without structure, the team spins on brainstorming, stakeholders pile on objections, and the first draft presentation becomes a data dump that convinces no one. The Pyramid Principle solves both problems: it gives you a logical scaffold *before* you have the answer (Clarity), and a persuasive presentation structure *after* you do (Conviction).

---

## What is the Pyramid Principle?

A **qualitative issue** is a business or personal question such as "Should we be doing X?", "How can we be more successful at Y?", or "How much support should we give Z?" — typically answered by words rather than numbers. _source: ch08 §'First, what is a qualitative issue?'_

The Pyramid Principle is a technique for structuring thinking into a hierarchical pyramid of sufficient conditions. It is used at two stages:

- **Clarity** — early in a project, to discover the underlying structure of a qualitative issue and quickly identify a convergent path to success, *before* you have the answer.
- **Conviction** — at the end of a project, to structure a presentation that convinces an audience of the validity of your recommendations, *after* you have the answer.

The structure itself persists across both stages. When facts arrive, you update the words inside the Post-its — the logic scaffold stays intact. _source: ch18 §Examples of Pyramids used throughout projects_

## Phase: Up — Building for Clarity

The Pyramid Principle is an **Up tool**, used during the Up phase to understand and structure a problem before data collection begins.

### Seven building tips

**Tip 1 — Rule 1: Write the most desirable outcome at the top.**
State the most desirable (or most difficult to achieve) outcome as an already-accomplished positive fact, not as a question. Moving from "how to achieve X" to "X has been achieved successfully" reduces tension and enables structured decomposition. _source: ch08 §'1. Write the most desirable outcome'_

**Tip 2 — Rule 2: Populate and structure using objections.**
Draft three Post-its representing conditions whose collective truth guarantees the top Post-it. Then ask: *"Can we think of a scenario where all three are true, and yet the one above is not automatically true?"* If yes, update the relevant Post-it to incorporate the objection. Objections are an abundant base metal; the Pyramid Principle converts them into a clear, logical, and rigorous structure. _source: ch08 §'2. Populate and structure'_

**Tip 3 — Rule 3: Follow MECE logic.**
- **Completely Exhaustive**: the sum total of the Post-its below covers all content in the one above.
- **Mutually Exclusive**: nothing is counted twice.

Completely Exhaustive is the more critical of the two. It is better to count something twice than to forget it altogether. _source: ch08 §'3. Follow a MECE logic'_

**Tip 4 — Rule 4: Gradually replace buzzwords with full sentences.**
Buzzwords ("guests", "logistics") are acceptable in the first draft to populate levels quickly. As the pyramid develops, replace each buzzword with a full sentence that captures the complete condition and reconciles different contributors' perspectives. **And: Rule 3: No fourth Post-it.** When a fourth condition is proposed at a level, write it down — then re-cut the four ideas into three Post-its. _source: ch08 §'4. Gradually replace buzzwords by full sentences'_

**Tip 5 — Rule 5: Use positive statements.**
Always write building blocks as positive statements, never questions or negatives. Questions and negative expressions ("Will all guests be there?" / "No guest will be missing") create anxiety and impede logical decomposition. Correct form: "All the guests are there and happy." _source: ch08 §'5. Use positive statements'_

**Tip 6 — Rule 6: Re-order by criticality.**
Within any group of conditions at the same level, place the most necessary condition on the left and the least necessary on the right. A story is told more effectively when conditions are ordered from most to least critical. _source: ch08 §'6. Re-order the building blocks'_

**Tip 7 — Rule 7: Smooth the pyramid.**
If the pyramid is skewed — more elements on one branch than others — either populate the sparse corner or restructure blocks to rebalance weight across all branches. _source: ch08 §'7. Continue to the next level down'_

### Four compounding benefits of a Clarity pyramid

1. **Early calmness** — converting the problem into a positive outcome statement reduces team anxiety immediately.
2. **Comprehensive workplan** — the bottom row of the pyramid (27, 81, 243 Post-its…) lists every concrete task that needs to be undertaken.
3. **Decision tree** — the pyramid visualised upside-down, with the desired outcome at the bottom. Results from the workplan trickle down to determine which specific version of the outcome is achieved.
4. **Structure for a convincing final presentation** — the same scaffold becomes the Conviction pyramid at project end.

## Phase: Push — Rebuilding for Conviction

At the end of the project, you apply the same pyramid for Conviction. The key shifts:

### From 'if' to 'because/therefore'
"Because" and "therefore" make a story compelling; "if" and "then" only make it credible. When facts are available, replace all conditional language with assertive, fact-based statements. _source: ch18 §Examples of Pyramids used throughout projects_

### Five end-of-project rules

**Rule 1: Structure your ideas in a pyramid form.**
One building block at the top, two or three below each block, continuing downward. _source: ch18 §1. Structure your ideas in a pyramid form_

**Rule 2: Present from the top down.**
State the conclusion first, then support it with details. At the point of Conviction you know the answer, so be crisp and assertive rather than building suspense. _source: ch18 §2. Present your ideas from the top down_

**Rule 3: Three sub-rules for every grouping.**
- Ideas at each level must be *summaries* of the ideas grouped below them.
- Ideas in each horizontal grouping must form either an **inductive** or a **deductive** argument — no mixed logic within a grouping.
- Ideas within a grouping must be in *logical order*: place the most important Post-it on the left, ordering the rest to tell the story most effectively for the specific audience.

_source: ch18 §3. Ideas in the pyramid have to obey three sub-rules_

**Rule 4: Check vertical relationships.**
Verify using "why / because / and / therefore": moving down answers "why?"; moving up produces "therefore". _source: ch18 §4. Make sure the vertical relationships work_

**Rule 5: Check horizontal relationships.**
- **Deductive grouping**: verify that the second Post-it comments on (refers back to) the first.
- **Inductive grouping**: verify that all Post-its can be summarised as a single plural statement.

_source: ch18 §5. Make sure the horizontal relationships work_

### Deductive vs. inductive groupings

| Grouping type | Logic | Horizontal test |
|---|---|---|
| **Deductive** | Because A and B, therefore C | Second Post-it must comment on the first |
| **Inductive** | A, B, C are all examples of X | All Post-its summarisable as one plural |

### Adland Swagger — making Conviction stick

Following Minto's rules early in a project is useful; following them at the end is *essential* for your story to prove compelling. Augment the end-of-project pyramid with three Adland Swagger elements:

- **Three-Word Summary** — distil the core idea into three (or four) words so colleagues can spread the word without access to the full business case.
- **One Simple Visual** — a single crisp image to increase memorability (over a third of people have a strong visual preference).
- **Two Human Anecdotes** — exactly two representative individual stories (customers, employees, or competitors). One is too risky (unrepresentative); three dilutes the message.

Formula: **1 visual, 2 people, 3 words.** _source: ch18 §IN SUMMARY (Adland Swagger)_

## Anti-Patterns

### Clarity-phase anti-patterns

**Pencil-on-paper** — Writing the pyramid in pencil on a fixed page causes you to become wedded to the first structure, preventing easy restructuring. **Fix:** Use Post-its (physical or digital) so you can chop and change, alter priorities, and bundle ideas more intelligently. Signals: reluctance to revise; first draft treated as final. _source: ch08 §'Strategic Pyramid examples'_

**Negative statements** — Phrasing building blocks as questions or negatives fuels anxiety and impedes logical decomposition. **Fix:** Rewrite as positive declarative statements. Signals: building block phrased as a question; operative word is "no" or "not". _source: ch08 §'5. Use positive statements'_

**Adding a fourth Post-it** — Simply adding a fourth Post-it ("no unforeseen circumstances") weakens the logical structure. **Fix:** Write down the fourth idea, then re-cut the four into three by finding a regrouping. Signals: a Post-it labelled "other" or "unforeseen circumstances"; four or more Post-its at the same level. _source: ch08 §'4. Gradually replace buzzwords by full sentences'_

**Buzzword-only Post-its** — Keeping building blocks as single buzzwords narrows interpretation and makes it harder to decompose to the next level. **Fix:** Gradually replace each buzzword with a full sentence. Signals: single-word or two-word Post-its in mid or lower pyramid levels; difficulty generating sub-conditions for a node. _source: ch08 §'4. Gradually replace buzzwords by full sentences'_

### Conviction-phase anti-patterns

**'If' language at end of project** — Retaining hypothetical "if … then" framing when facts are already available signals a lack of conviction. **Fix:** Replace all "if" Post-its with assertive, fact-based statements. Signals: presentation contains "if" before key claims; conclusions framed as conditional. _source: ch18 opening paragraphs_

**Single anecdote** — One individual's story risks invalidating the case if that person turns out to be unrepresentative. **Fix:** Use exactly two representative individuals. _source: ch18 §TWO HUMAN ANECDOTES_

**Three or more anecdotes** — Three or more stories introduce too much variety, diluting the core message. **Fix:** Trim to exactly two representative anecdotes. _source: ch18 §TWO HUMAN ANECDOTES_

## When to Use / When NOT to Use

**Use Clarity pyramid when:**
- You are facing a qualitative issue (answered in words rather than numbers).
- You do not yet know the answer to a "How?" or "Should we?" question.
- You need to quickly generate ideas and structure for a complex problem.
- You are under stakeholder pressure or a tight deadline.

**Use Conviction pyramid when:**
- You have data-backed conclusions and need to present them persuasively to a decision-making audience.

**Do NOT use the Clarity pyramid when:**
- You already have a clear, data-backed answer — switch directly to the Conviction mode.

**Do NOT use the Conviction pyramid when:**
- The project is still in its exploratory phase and the team hasn't converged on a recommendation — using assertive "because/therefore" language prematurely will erode credibility if the facts later change.

**MECE tie-breaker:** When Mutually Exclusive and Completely Exhaustive conflict, always prioritise Completely Exhaustive — it is better to count something twice than to forget it altogether.

**Stopping rule for tightening a level:** Stop when you cannot think of any scenario under which the three Post-its at that level are all true and yet the Post-it above is not automatically true. _source: ch08 §'4. Gradually replace buzzwords by full sentences'_

## Code Examples

### Clarity pyramid — birthday party example (early project)

```
TOP:  "The party is a great success for everyone"
        |
   ┌────┼────┐
   ▼    ▼    ▼
"All the   "The food    "The house
 guests    and drinks   is ready and
 are there  are great"   safe"
 and happy"
```

After counter-scenario check on the left node ("What if guests are there but unhappy?"), the Post-it tightens to:
```
"All the guests are there, comfortable, and having fun"
```

### Conviction pyramid — language shift example

```
EARLY (Clarity)          →   LATE (Conviction)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────
"IF all guests arrive"   →   "Because all guests arrived"
"IF food is adequate"    →   "Because food exceeded expectations"
"THEN the party is       →   "THEREFORE the party was
 likely a success"            a great success"
```

### Adland Swagger formula

```
COMPELLING STORY = Pyramid (Conviction rules)
                 + 1 Simple Visual
                 + 2 Human Anecdotes
                 + 3-Word Summary
```

### MECE check (deductive vs. inductive horizontal test)

```ruby
# Deductive grouping — second idea comments on the first
grouping = [
  "Our cost base is 30% above the market average",          # first
  "Competitors are using automation we have not adopted",   # second (comments on first)
  # => Therefore: "We must close the automation gap to survive"
]

# Inductive grouping — all summarisable as a plural
grouping = [
  "Customer NPS fell 12 points in Q3",
  "Repeat purchase rate dropped 8%",
  "Average basket size is down 15%",
  # => "Customer engagement metrics are deteriorating across the board"
]
```
