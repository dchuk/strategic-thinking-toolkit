# The Mutation Game

## Problem Framing

Innovation pipelines in most organisations rely on one dominant source: watching competitors. The problem is that you only see the **10% of ideas that survived their iceberg of failure** — the visible successes of rivals who tried ten things, buried nine, and publicised one. Copy that visible tip and you inherit a narrow, already-commoditised idea space.

Customer research helps, but structured self-mutation — starting from *what you already are* and systematically varying it — is the third source most teams skip entirely. The Mutation Game fills that gap.

The technique belongs to the **Up phase** of the Rollercoaster: the divergent moment when the goal is to generate as many strategic options as possible before selecting among them. It is not a prioritisation tool; it is a creativity engine.

> "The aim of the Mutation Game is to change little in the description of a business object while achieving a big change on the outside — minimal changes on the inside, for maximum impact on the outside." _source: ch10_

---

## What Is the Mutation Game?

The Mutation Game is a structured business ideation technique that intentionally replicates the characteristics and benefits of natural selection, applied to ideas rather than biological entities. Darwin's insight: slight variations (mutations), if useful, are preserved. The Mutation Game artificially creates many mutations and identifies which survive testing.

**Three sources of strategic ideas** — and where the Mutation Game fits:

1. **Copy the competition** — fast, but limited to visible successes
2. **Ask/observe customers** (demand side) — rich, but resource-intensive
3. **Mutate yourself** — start from the organisation as it is today ← the Mutation Game

> "Keeping up with competitors' successes is good, but adding other sources of inspiration (customers, self-mutation) is even better." _source: ch10_

The technique is entirely words-based, requiring no complex maths or finance, so **everyone in the company can play at every level, in every function, on every issue**.

## The Four Steps

### Step 1 — Craft a Description (5 min)

Pick a meaningful string of **five to eight components** that describe the business object to mutate *as it stands today*. Write one component per Post-it in a column.

- Use one word per component where possible (multi-word components are allowed when necessary, e.g., "for a short ride")
- Avoid bridging words ("and", "the") — every Post-it must carry distinct meaning
- The object can be a process, product, industry, initiative, or company

**Demand side vs. supply side:** A business object can be described starting from the customer perspective (demand side) or the producer/expert perspective (supply side). Prefer the demand-side formulation — starting with the customer usually expands the mind more. Try both; they offer different creative benefits.

### Step 2 — Generate Alternative Components (15 min)

For each component Post-it in column 1, generate **two to four alternative variants** and place them as new Post-its in columns 2, 3, and 4.

**Rule 1:** Aim for a rectangular grid — **five to eight rows × two to four columns** — with as few blank spaces as possible. _source: ch10 §Tip 1_

**Rule 2:** Slightly silly ideas during this phase are absolutely fine — they are a great way to explore the richness of variants available. Non-judgement preserves creative options that might trigger valuable mutations later.

**Rule 3:** Start large to stimulate creativity, then shrink back to ensure a high ratio of practical ideas.

### Step 3 — Pick Mutations and Generate Ideas (30 min)

A **mutation** is formed by selecting one Post-it per row. The result is a new descriptive sentence for the business object.

**Label each mutation with its unique number:** a string of column-position digits, one per row. For example, `112` means row 1 → column 1, row 2 → column 1, row 3 → column 2. This facilitates tracking and recollection of the idea source.

**Rule 4:** Go through **around 50 mutations**, spending 10–20 seconds per mutation, to trigger ideas. If a mutation yields nothing, move on immediately. _source: ch10 §Pick Mutations and Generate Ideas_

**Rule 5:** Start with mutations close to the original (mostly column 1 selections), then explore wilder combinations to loosen inhibitions, then return to near-original combinations for practical ideas. _source: ch10 §Tip 2_

**Rule 6:** Use the app at `www.strategic.how/mutation` to properly randomise mutations — manual selection unconsciously drifts toward meaningful sentences, skewing away from truly random combinations and missing breakthrough ideas. _source: ch10 §Tip 4_

> "The more mutations you explore, the more likely it is that one of them will prove useful and survive natural selection." _source: ch10_

### Step 4 — Package Your Best Ideas (10 min)

**Rule 7:** Summarise each best idea in **two to four words**. Attach the unique mutation number that triggered it for later recollection and explanation. _source: ch10 §Package your Best Ideas_

## The Mutation Grid

```
COMPONENT          | ORIGINAL (col 1)  | VARIANT 2        | VARIANT 3       | VARIANT 4
-------------------|-------------------|------------------|-----------------|------------------
[Row 1 component]  | original word     | alternative A    | alternative B   | alternative C
[Row 2 component]  | original word     | alternative A    | alternative B   |
[Row 3 component]  | original word     | alternative A    | alternative B   | alternative C
[Row 4 component]  | original word     | alternative A    | alternative B   |
[Row 5 component]  | original word     | alternative A    | alternative B   | alternative C
```

Selecting one cell per row produces a unique mutation. The unique number `11312` (five rows) means: row 1 col 1, row 2 col 1, row 3 col 3, row 4 col 1, row 5 col 2.

**Ideal grid shape:** 5–8 rows × 2–4 columns ≈ potential combinations in the hundreds to thousands. Exploring ~50 is a practical minimum.

## Facilitation Rules

**Rule 8 (group size):** Run the Mutation Game with **four to six participants** for a typical 60-minute session. Fewer people run out of steam; the long tail of valuable mutations requires group energy. _source: ch10 §The Mutation Game in practice_

**Rule 9 (divergent frame):** Make explicit at the start that this is a divergent thinking session. Evaluation and selection happen *after* — not during — mutation exploration.

**Rule 10 (two descriptions):** Facilitate at least one demand-side and one supply-side description of the same business object. Run mutations on the one that feels most expansive, but keep the other for a second pass.

## The Natural Selection Analogy

The biological parallel is instructive:

| Biology | Mutation Game |
|---|---|
| Gene | Component of descriptive sentence |
| Mutation | Substituted variant in one or more components |
| Population | The full mutation grid |
| Natural selection | Testing ideas against market reality |
| Survival | Ideas worth packaging and pursuing |

Natural selection does not plan which mutations survive — it tests them all. The Mutation Game mimics this: **volume of exploration increases the probability of finding a valuable mutation**. Unconscious human selection (picking combinations that "look right") defeats the purpose. Use randomisation when possible.

## Worked Example: Reception Area

A company examining its reception area might craft this supply-side description:

```
[a company] [welcomes] [clients] [in] [its office]
```

Demand-side reframe:
```
[visitors] [arrive at] [a building] [to meet] [staff]
```

Generating 2–4 variants per component fills the grid. Mutation `21312` might combine:
- "customers" (variant 2 of row 1)
- "arrive at" (original row 2)
- "a kiosk" (variant 3 of row 3)
- "to meet" (original row 4)
- "a virtual agent" (variant 2 of row 5)

→ Idea seed: *automated check-in kiosk connecting visitors directly to a virtual agent*. Packaged in two words: **"Virtual Welcome"** — mutation `21312`.

## When to Use / When NOT to Use

**Use the Mutation Game when:**
- You are in the **Up phase** of strategic thinking and need to generate a large number of options quickly
- You are exploring new processes, products, businesses, or industries
- You want to include participants at every level and function without specialist training
- Your innovation pipeline relies too heavily on competitor copying
- You need to diversify beyond customer research alone

**Do NOT use the Mutation Game when:**
- You are in the **Down phase** — you already have options and need to select, prioritise, or validate among them (use Payoff Profiles Matrix or Lean Startup instead)
- The business object is so tightly constrained by regulation or hard technical limits that component substitution is meaningless
- The group is too small (fewer than 4) or the session too short (<30 min) to reach 50 mutations

## Anti-Patterns

### Relying Solely on Copying Competitors
Using competitor imitation as the only source of business innovation limits you to the visible 10% of ideas that survived their iceberg of failure.
**Fix:** Supplement competitor monitoring with demand-side inspiration and self-mutation via the Mutation Game.
**Signals:** No internal ideation process; innovation pipeline mirrors competitors' recent moves; no customer-originated ideas.

### Unconsciously Crafting Meaningful Sentences (Unconscious Skew)
When picking mutations by hand, people unconsciously select combinations that read as coherent sentences, skewing away from truly random combinations and missing breakthrough ideas.
**Fix:** Use the app at `www.strategic.how/mutation` to properly randomise mutations — closer to true natural selection.
**Signals:** All explored mutations look logical and incremental; no surprising or puzzling mutations examined; team gravitating toward comfortable combinations.

### Stopping Too Early (Too-Few-Mutations)
Individuals run out of steam after eight to ten ideas, missing the long tail of valuable mutations.
**Fix:** Play with four to six people and systematically go through around 50 mutations.
**Signals:** Session ends after fewer than 10 ideas; only one or two people generating ideas; grid not fully traversed.

## Code Examples

### 60-Minute Session Agenda

```
MUTATION GAME — 60-MINUTE SESSION PLAN

Step 1 — CRAFT A DESCRIPTION           (5 min)
  • Choose business object to mutate
  • Write 5–8 components, one Post-it each, in a column
  • Try demand-side AND supply-side formulations
  • Remove bridging words; every Post-it must carry meaning

Step 2 — GENERATE ALTERNATIVE COMPONENTS (15 min)
  • For each component, write 2–4 alternative variants
  • Place variants as Post-its in columns 2, 3, 4
  • Target: rectangular grid (5–8 rows × 2–4 cols), no blanks
  • Silly variants welcome — suspend judgement

Step 3 — PICK MUTATIONS AND GENERATE IDEAS (30 min)
  • Select 1 Post-it per row → 1 mutation
  • Label mutation with unique number (column digits, one per row)
  • Target: ~50 mutations at 10–20 sec each
  • Start near-original, go wild to loosen up, return near-original
  • Use app for true randomisation (www.strategic.how/mutation)
  • Record all ideas; skip blank mutations immediately

Step 4 — PACKAGE YOUR BEST IDEAS        (10 min)
  • Summarise each idea in 2–4 words
  • Attach unique mutation number to each idea
```

```

Row 1:  taxi        | uber          | bus

Row 4:  to the city | to an airport | door-to-door

  Row1→col1: taxi
  Row2→col1: takes
  Row3→col1: a passenger
  Row4→col2: to an airport
  Row5→col1: for a fare

  Row2→col3: moves

  Row4→col1: to the city
  Row5→col3: on subscription
```
