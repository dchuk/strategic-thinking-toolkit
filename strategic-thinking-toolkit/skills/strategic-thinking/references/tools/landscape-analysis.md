# Landscape Analysis

## Problem Framing

You have just finished the Up phase of the Rollercoaster of Strategic Thinking. The team has generated 10–20 ideas using tools like the Happy Line or the Mutation Game. Now a familiar trap opens: someone suggests building a comprehensive data inventory — pulling every report, running a big survey, loading spreadsheets — before deciding which ideas deserve investment.

The result is the **Submarine Path of Analytical Research**: crawling dataset after dataset in sequence without surfacing to test ideas, consuming weeks of time with diminishing returns. By the time the data arrives, the strategic window may have closed, or the accumulated data has subtly biased thinking before the key questions were even framed.

Landscape Analysis is the Down-phase antidote. It is a disciplined, visual, four-quadrant framework that forces you to identify what you need to know *before* you gather anything, then collect only those targeted data points as fast as possible.

> "A good strategist should always prefer limited data gathered quickly over a comprehensive dataset gathered too late." _source: ch13 §'Five key data points'_

---

## The Five Key Data Points Principle

At the heart of Landscape Analysis sits a deceptively simple claim: on any strategic issue, there are **only five critical data points** needed.

- **Two** are already at hand — you have them now.
- **Two** can be gathered through targeted research (surveys, interviews, modelling).
- **One** will always remain elusive — because strategy concerns the future.

The third category is the most important to accept. The **Elusive Fifth Data Point** means you can never convince anyone of a new strategic idea through data alone. Once you accept this, the implication is liberating: *stop trying to achieve perfect proof and start optimising for speed.*

> "You can never convince anyone of a new strategic idea through data alone, because you will always be one data point short of the perfect proof. Once you accept this, your problem-solving life becomes simpler — it's all about speed." _source: ch13 §'Five key data points'_

### On-demand vs. On-supply Data

The book draws an analogy from entertainment. On-supply logic means accumulating large data inventories first and choosing what to use afterwards — the old broadcast model. On-demand logic means identifying exactly what you need and fetching only that — the streaming model. A good strategist always uses on-demand logic.

Rule 1: Think before gathering. Refuse to manipulate data until it is strictly necessary. Keep thinking until you know exactly what data you want *and what result you expect*. Both expected and unexpected results are valuable — the faster the findings, the better.

Rule 2: Stakeholders bring their own thinking. Data provided to stakeholders does not need to be as comprehensive as feared. Sharing initial options early at the Point of Clarity gives stakeholders time to settle their anxieties around ideas before the final recommendation is presented.

## The Four Quadrants of Landscape Analysis

Landscape Analysis organises all data-gathering across a 2×2 framework:

|  | **Macro** | **Micro** |
|---|---|---|
| **Demand** | Market Analysis | Customer Analysis |
| **Supply** | Industry Analysis | Company Analysis |

Each quadrant has a dedicated visual tool.

---

## The Four Visual Tools

### Mekko (Market Analysis — Demand/Macro)

A **Mekko** (named after Marimekko) displays three cuts of a dataset in one single chart:

- **Horizontal cut**: column widths are proportional to each segment's share of the total.
- **Vertical cut**: each column is split vertically into sub-segments proportional to their share within that segment.
- **Colour**: groups items that belong together across segments.

**Construction rules:**

- Rule 3: Never use a Mekko for data series that change sign (e.g. profits that include losses). All values within a series must be either all positive or all negative.
- Rule 4: When presenting a Mekko to a new audience, always guide their eyes around the chart first — total rectangle → horizontal widths → vertical splits → colour coding — *before* explaining the insights.
- Rule 5: When validating a new business idea, always construct the three or four most useful Mekkos you can think of to help size up and visualise the challenges the idea faces.

```
Mekko construction sequence:
1. Total rectangle = total market (or dataset)
2. Column width  ∝ segment share of total
3. Column height splits ∝ sub-segment share within segment
4. Colour = cross-segment grouping dimension
5. Present: rectangle → widths → splits → colours → insight
```

---

### GPS Chart (Industry Analysis — Supply/Macro)

A **GPS Chart** is a bubble chart plotting all competitors in an industry on three dimensions simultaneously:

- **Horizontal axis** — Growth
- **Vertical axis** — Profitability
- **Bubble diameter** — Size (sales)

Rule 6: Set both axes at the industry averages. The horizontal axis crosses the vertical at average industry profitability; the vertical axis crosses the horizontal at average industry growth.

This creates four interpretive quadrants:

| Quadrant | Growth | Profitability | Archetype |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fading** | Below avg | Below avg | Declining players |
| **Lifestyle** | Below avg | Above avg | Profitable but stagnant |
| **Buying** | Above avg | Below avg | Growing but not profitable |
| **Champions** | Above avg | Above avg | Draw strategic inspiration here |

**Anti-pattern — Anecdotal Competitor Benchmarking:** Drawing inspiration from competitors prominent in internal anecdotes (former employees, PR stories) rather than from objectively successful ones. Fix: build the GPS Chart and focus on the Champions quadrant.

Rule 7: When benchmarking, always ask whether the Champions in your industry would do it, and assess the impact on profitability and growth.

```
GPS Chart axis setup:
- x-axis: revenue growth (%) | x-axis crosses y at avg-industry-growth
- y-axis: operating profitability (%) | y-axis crosses x at avg-industry-profitability
- bubble size: sales volume / revenue
- label: competitor name
- quadrant labels: Fading | Lifestyle | Buying | Champions
```

### Conversion Waterfall (Customer Analysis — Demand/Micro)

A **Conversion Waterfall** tracks what percentage of a target population passes through sequential conversion steps (e.g. Awareness → Knowledge → Liking → Preference → Visit → Purchase), identifying where customers are lost ('leakage') and pointing to remedial actions.

The diagnostic chain is: **where** is the drop → **why** does it happen → **what** should be done.

Rule 8: When validating a new business idea, always construct the Conversion Waterfall to discover how the idea will help fix existing leakage — or whether it doesn't help at all.

```
Conversion Waterfall structure:
  Target population: 100%
       │
  Awareness:    e.g. 72%  ← primary research (survey / focus group)
       │
  Knowledge:    e.g. 58%
       │
  Liking:       e.g. 41%  ← leakage point: −17pp
       │
  Preference:   e.g. 29%
       │
  Visit:        e.g. 18%  ← leakage point: −11pp
       │
  Purchase:     e.g. 11%
```

Compare the waterfall across markets, segments, or scenarios to identify where a proposed idea is genuinely additive.

### Line Profitability — XLP (Company Analysis — Supply/Micro)

**Line Profitability (XLP)** progressively allocates unallocated fixed costs to products, customers, or suppliers, revealing true profitability below the gross margin line.

The X in XLP is a placeholder:
- **PLP** = Product Line Profitability
- **CLP** = Customer Line Profitability
- **SLP** = Supplier Line Profitability

The output is a cascade of gross margin layers:

```
GM  = Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold
      (standard gross margin)

GM1 = GM  − allocated fixed cost #1
      (e.g. store rent/depreciation allocated by square footage)

GM2 = GM1 − allocated fixed cost #2
      (e.g. call centre cost allocated by minutes of interaction)

…and so on for further cost categories.
```

Rule 9: In any XLP analysis, allocate each category of fixed costs in proportion to the actual amount consumed by each product/customer/supplier, using an appropriate allocation key (e.g. square footage for rent, minutes of interaction for call centres).

Rule 10: When validating a new business idea, always think through the XLP angles. Even without a full analysis, working out the 'hidden consumption' gives a better feel for the likely true impact.

**Anti-pattern — Gross-Margin-Only Resource Allocation:** Making resource allocation decisions based solely on gross margin, ignoring hidden consumption of unallocated fixed costs. Fix: perform XLP to compute GM1 and GM2, revealing true profitability.

## Sharp Data: The Underlying Philosophy

The 'Sharp Data' school advocates collecting **as little data as needed, as fast as possible**, rather than assembling comprehensive datasets. The sequence is always: identify the right questions → identify what you already have → gather only what's missing.

**Anti-pattern — Big Data Hoarding:** Gathering huge datasets before knowing what questions to ask. Signals: the team is collecting "everything we can think of" before framing questions; analysis is delayed waiting for a comprehensive dataset. Fix: adopt the sharp data approach.

### Sharpening a Primary Research Question

When primary research is needed, apply iterative question refinement:

1. Write a first version based on what you want to know.
2. Check for distorting filters (respondent ambition, self-interest); rewrite to remove.
3. Check for cognitive bias (self-awareness, over-confidence); rewrite for objective phrasing.
4. Check for unfocused answers; rewrite to elicit concise, actionable responses.
5. Finalise the single sharpest question.
6. Select the smallest number of most predictive respondents — focus on the group **most likely to find the change hardest** (worst-case scenario), excluding those with conflicting personal interests.
7. Gather responses within 24 hours.

> "'Sharp data' always trumps 'big data'. A small, precisely targeted sample of the right respondents is more convincing and actionable than a large but unfocused dataset." _source: ch13 §'Landscape Analysis example'_

**Anti-pattern — Broad Survey Design:** Asking as many questions of as many respondents as possible. Fix: reduce to the single most critical question through iterative refinement; select the worst-case respondents; gather within 24 hours.

Rule 11: When selecting survey respondents, exclude those who are directly threatened by the proposed change (e.g. those who would be made redundant), as their views introduce a different bias.

## The Full Landscape Analysis Workflow

**Trigger:** You have a shortlist of strategic options from the Up phase and need to validate or invalidate them with data.

1. List the ideas from the Up phase; identify the two or three that most demand validation.
2. For each idea, identify the five key data points needed to validate or invalidate it — think before gathering any data.
3. Determine which two data points you already have.
4. Identify which two can be gathered through targeted research.
5. Acknowledge the elusive fifth data point and plan accordingly.
6. Map your five data points across the Four Quadrants to check for blind spots.
7. Build the relevant visual tool for each populated quadrant: Mekko(s), GPS Chart, Conversion Waterfall, XLP.
8. Gather only the missing data, as fast as possible, using the sharp data principle.
9. Analyse results and rate each strategic idea as yes / maybe / no.
10. Where demand-side data is thin, decide whether to interview customers or run a small-scale test (see Lean Startup).

> "Actions speak louder than words — test ideas on customers rather than merely interviewing them when possible. Interview data on customer intent is weaker than behavioural data from a small-scale test." _source: ch13 §'Landscape Analysis example'_

## When to Use / When NOT to Use

### Use Landscape Analysis when:
- You have completed the Up phase and have a pool of strategic options that need validation or invalidation.
- Decisions are being made based on intuition, anecdote, or gross margin alone.
- The team is tempted to start a large data-gathering exercise without first framing the questions.
- You need to benchmark competitors objectively (GPS Chart) or diagnose customer leakage (Conversion Waterfall).
- You suspect products or customers are consuming hidden fixed costs not reflected in gross margin (XLP).

### Do NOT use (or modify approach) when:
- You have not yet generated strategic options — Landscape Analysis validates options; it does not generate them. Run the Happy Line or Mutation Game first.
- The strategic question is purely qualitative — use the Pyramid Principle instead.
- You lack sequential conversion steps in the customer journey — the Conversion Waterfall requires a defined funnel.
- Profitability data is unavailable for competitors — the GPS Chart is still worth attempting, though incomplete.
- Time and budget do not permit a full XLP analysis — at minimum, think through the XLP angles and estimate hidden consumption.

### Mekko Construction Checklist

```
Mekko validation checklist:
□ All values same sign (no sign changes)          → Rule: mekko-no-sign-change
□ Column widths ∝ segment share of total
□ Vertical splits ∝ sub-segment share within column
□ Colour codes are consistent across columns
□ Presentation order: rectangle → widths → splits → colours → insight
                                                   → Rule: mekko-guide-audience
```

### GPS Chart Setup

```
GPS Chart axis configuration:
  x-axis label : "Revenue Growth (%)"
  y-axis label : "Operating Profitability (%)"
  x-axis zero  : set at AVERAGE industry profitability   ← Rule: gps-axes-at-average
  y-axis zero  : set at AVERAGE industry growth          ← Rule: gps-axes-at-average
  bubble size  : proportional to company revenue/sales

Quadrant labels (clockwise from top-right):
  Champions  (top-right)   : above-avg growth, above-avg profitability
  Buying     (bottom-right): above-avg growth, below-avg profitability
  Fading     (bottom-left) : below-avg growth, below-avg profitability
  Lifestyle  (top-left)    : below-avg growth, above-avg profitability
```

### XLP Cascade

```
Product / Customer / Supplier Line Profitability:

               Product A   Product B   Product C
Revenue          1,000         600         400
COGS              (600)       (300)       (280)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────
GM                 400         300         120    ← gross margin only

Allocation key #1: square footage (floor space)
  Rent allocated   (180)        (60)        (60)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────
GM1                220         240          60    ← after space costs

Allocation key #2: call centre minutes
  Call centre       (20)        (10)        (70)  ← hidden consumption
─────────────────────────────────────────────────
GM2                200         230         (10)   ← Product C is loss-making
```

### Sharp Data Survey Sharpening

```
Iteration 1 (distorted by ambition):
  "Would you like to manage a larger team in the new structure?"
  Problem: introduces career-ambition filter → remove

Iteration 2 (cognitive bias):
  "Do you think you would cope with the new reporting process?"
  Problem: self-awareness / over-confidence bias → rewrite

Iteration 3 (final sharp question):
  "In the new structure, how many hours per week would the
   reporting process require from you?"
  ✓ Objective, quantifiable, actionable
```
