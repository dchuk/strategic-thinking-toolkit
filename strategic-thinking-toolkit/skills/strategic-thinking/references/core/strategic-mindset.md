# The Strategic Mindset

## Problem Framing

A common trap for smart, experienced professionals: they face a genuinely difficult, future-oriented problem and reach for the same tool they always use — more data, more analysis, more expert opinion. The result is paralysis, endless research with no resolution, or a solution that convinces no one.

The strategic mindset is the fix. It is not a new set of tools; it is a fundamentally different orientation to problem-solving that recognises when the analytical route is the *wrong* route, and that equips you to choose a better one. As the book puts it: "Being strategic is a skill, and it can be learned." _source: ch04 opening paragraphs_

---

## What Makes a Problem "Truly Strategic"

Not every hard problem requires strategic thinking. The book gives six concrete conditions that, when present together, signal that you need to think strategically:

**Rule 1: Apply the six-condition test.** Engage full strategic thinking when a problem is:

1. **Big** — its scope or consequences matter at an organisational level
2. **In the future** — the answer does not yet exist in current data
3. **Never been done before** — no established playbook applies
4. **Data-sparse** — very little reliable data will ever be available
5. **Not just a matter of taste** — there is a better and a worse answer, not merely a preferred one
6. **Requiring proof** — many stakeholders must be convinced before anything moves

_source: ch21 opening section_

When fewer than three of these conditions are present, a Staircase (expert) or Submarine (analytical) route is probably faster and more appropriate. Save the Rollercoaster for problems that genuinely need it.

## Mindset vs. Toolset

The book draws a sharp distinction:

> "Being Strategic Is a Mindset, Not a Toolset." _source: ch21 §2_

The **toolset** is the Up–Down–Push framework and its ten techniques. The **mindset** is the orientation that makes you reach for those tools at the right moment and in the right order.

Participants consistently report that acquiring a radically different mindset — not accumulating more tools — is the true turning point in becoming strategic. The penny-drops moment arrives when a person realises that everyday analytical routes do not work for big, future problems. _source: ch21 §2_

**Mindset** addresses how one deals with future uncertainty. **Toolset** provides structured techniques for generating, eliminating, and packaging strategic solutions. Both are required; mindset comes first.

## The Future-as-Creation Principle

The single most important mindset shift is accepting this principle:

> "The future cannot be analysed; it can only be created." _source: ch06 §Submarine of Analytical Research_

Hard facts exist only in the past and present. The more strategic the issue, the less applicable purely data-driven approaches become. Applying analytical force to a future problem before generating possible futures leads to paralysis and endless data-seeking with no resolution. _source: ch21 §4_

This has a practical corollary for sequencing:

> "To be strategic, first be creative, then be analytical — not the other way around." _source: ch06 §Rollercoaster of Strategic Thinking_

**Rule 2: Create before you analyse.** Always generate possible futures first (Up), then use data to validate or eliminate them (Down). Never attempt to analyse your way to an idea.

The data implication is equally important: "Credible strategic solutions can and should be crafted out of very little actual data." Each Up technique is compared to a camel in the desert — taking you far on very little water. _source: ch04 §Up description_

And yet: "You don't need real data to have real ideas, but you do need real data to have real solutions." An idea is not a solution until tested against hard evidence. _source: ch21 §5 and §6_

## The Internal Dialogue Model (Coach A / B / C)

Strategic thinking is partly an inner game. The book identifies three modes of internal dialogue, drawn from NLP:

| Coach | Stance | Effect on Search |
|-------|--------|-----------------|
| **Coach A** | Highly supportive — "That's great, stop here!" | Stops search prematurely out of satisfaction |
| **Coach B** | Balanced — credits effort, maintains tension | Keeps the search alive; optimal |
| **Coach C** | Harshly critical — "That's terrible, give up!" | Stops search prematurely out of dejection |

**Rule 3: Maintain a Coach B internal dialogue.** The optimal stance gives credit for effort to date while inserting just enough tension to continue searching. Both extremes cause the search for better options to stop too soon. _source: ch20 §Tip 1_

**Rule 4: Ask for two solutions, not one.** When facing a complex strategic problem, frame the inner question as: *"What are two widely different solutions to this problem?"* — not *"Is there a solution?"* This frames the problem as solvable rather than open-ended. _source: ch20 §Tip 1_

The Third Solution principle follows directly: on complex problems the first two options generated are rarely the best. Deliberately seeking a third option dramatically increases the chances of finding a superior solution — often as a synthesis of earlier extremes. The act of reaching a third solution also primes the mind to recognise that further options exist. _source: ch20 §Tip 1_

## Temperament Archetypes: Tigger, Eeyore, and Pooh

The book uses three characters from A. A. Milne to describe problem-solving personalities:

| Archetype | Dominant Mode | Peak Phase |
|-----------|--------------|------------|
| **Tigger** | Divergent, impulsive, idea-generating | Up (generative) and final Push |
| **Eeyore** | Cautious, flaw-spotting, sceptical | Down (evaluative) |
| **Pooh** | Balanced, even-keeled, facilitative | All phases |

These are not fixed personality labels but dominant tendencies that can be recognised and deployed deliberately.

**Rule 5: Aim for at least a third Tiggers in any strategic taskforce, and preferably a third each of Tiggers, Eeyores, and Poohs.** Each temperament contributes a distinct and complementary function: impulse (Tigger), flaw-spotting (Eeyore), and even-keeled facilitation (Pooh). _source: ch20 §Tip 2_

### Divergent and Convergent Thinking

Tigger energy is *divergent*: it relishes differences, invites complexity, and encourages more options. Eeyore energy is *convergent*: it aims for one outcome quickly and is productivity-minded. Effective strategic sessions alternate between both modes multiple times. Beware of **Bike-shedding** — the syndrome where groups devote disproportionate time to trivial, familiar matters while glossing over the complex, important ones, because everyone feels competent to opine on the former. _source: ch20 §Tip 4_

## Team Composition and Session Principles

The mindset operates in a social context. The book provides concrete structural rules:

**Rule 6 (Small Up, Large Down).** Use small teams for the Up (generative) phase; use large teams for the Down (evaluative) phase. Small teams generate ideas faster because every participant's contribution is noticed. Large teams spot flaws faster because more expertise is present. _source: ch20 §Tip 2_

**Rule 7 (Taskforce size).** Assemble 12–16 people for a full strategic taskforce. Split into sub-groups of 4–5 for Up sessions. In a startup, 4–6 contributors may suffice. _source: ch20 §Tip 2_

**Rule 8 (Session structure).** Two-meeting format:
- **Meeting 1 (Up):** Parallel small teams, randomly mixing functions and seniority, at least one Tigger per team, teams swapping after 15–20 minutes. Circulate a half-page problem memo beforehand.
- **Between meetings:** Circulate a memo capturing the 5–10 best ideas — a few lines each, a catchy name, a sense of the upside.
- **Meeting 2 (Down):** Full plenary. Capture and act on all objections immediately.
- **After Meeting 2:** Task three sub-groups with progressing the final three best ideas, one idea per sub-group.

_source: ch20 §Tip 2_

**Rule 9 (Vote before debate).** In the convergent phase, have all participants vote silently with sticky dots before opening debate. This surfaces majority consensus rapidly, focuses debate only on material disagreements, and eliminates time wasted on issues the group broadly agrees on. _source: ch20 §Tip 4_

**Rule 10 (Same outcome, pick one).** If two competing options produce the same outcome, stop debating and pick one — use a coin flip if necessary. Only carry both forward if they produce *different* outcomes. _source: ch20 §Tip 4_

## When to Use / When NOT to Use

**Use the strategic mindset framework when:**
- All or most of the six conditions are present (big, future, novel, data-sparse, non-taste-based, requires proof)
- You notice yourself defaulting to data-gathering as a first move on a future-oriented problem
- A team is stuck in analysis paralysis or keeps asking for more research before deciding
- You are assembling a taskforce and need to balance temperament types deliberately
- An internal dialogue is stopping the search for options prematurely (Coach A or C taking over)

**Do NOT apply these principles when:**
- The answer is already known before you start — use the Staircase (expert execution) instead
- The problem is primarily a matter of past or present fact — the Submarine (analytical research) is faster and more appropriate
- The issue is genuinely a matter of taste with no objectively better answer — the six-condition test will flag this
- Time constraints make a two-meeting format impossible — compress, but preserve the Up-before-Down sequence at minimum

## Code Examples

The six-condition checklist and the Coach A/B/C model are best applied as structured prompts. Below is a facilitator-ready version of both.

```
# Six-Condition Strategic Test
Is this problem truly strategic? Score each condition (Y/N):

1. [ ] Big — organisational-level scope or consequences
2. [ ] Future — answer does not exist in current data
3. [ ] Novel — no established playbook applies
4. [ ] Data-sparse — very little reliable data will ever be available
5. [ ] Non-taste-based — a better and a worse answer exists
6. [ ] Proof-required — many stakeholders must be convinced

Result:
  5–6 Y → Full Rollercoaster (Up–Down–Push)
  3–4 Y → Consider Helicopter or partial Rollercoaster
  0–2 Y → Staircase or Submarine is faster; strategic thinking is overhead
```

```
# Internal Dialogue Check (Coach A / B / C)
Before closing on an option, ask yourself:

  Coach A signal (stop!): "This is great — we're done."
    → Counter: "What would a third, very different option look like?"

  Coach B signal (optimal): "Good progress — but is there something better?"
    → Action: Keep searching one more round.

  Coach C signal (stop!): "Nothing is working — this is hopeless."
    → Counter: "What are two widely different solutions to this problem?"

Target state: Coach B throughout the Up phase.
```

```
# Taskforce Composition Template
Total size: 12–16 (established org) | 4–6 (startup)

Up session sub-groups (4–5 per group):
  - Mix functions randomly (no silo clusters)
  - Mix seniority (no hierarchy clusters)
  - Guarantee ≥1 Tigger per sub-group
  - Rotate groups every 15–20 minutes

Temperament target:
  ~⅓ Tiggers  (divergent energy, idea impulse)
  ~⅓ Eeyores  (convergent energy, flaw-spotting)
  ~⅓ Poohs    (balanced, facilitative)

Down session: full plenary — all 12–16 together
```
