# The Rollercoaster of Strategic Thinking

## Problem Framing

Most people default to one problem-solving approach for everything. Analysts reach for data; consultants reach for frameworks; experts reach for precedent. When a truly strategic issue arrives — big, future-oriented, unprecedented, data-scarce, requiring proof across many stakeholders — that default fails. The analyst drowns waiting for data that will never arrive. The expert finds no matching precedent. The result is either paralysis or a recommendation that nobody believes.

The Rollercoaster of Strategic Thinking is the answer: a deliberate, sequenced route that generates options creatively first, eliminates them analytically second, and packages the survivor for stakeholder Conviction last. It is the unifying mental model behind the entire Up–Down–Push framework.

---

## The Problem-Solving Map: Four Corners

Every problem-solving journey starts at the **Complexity Corner** (bottom-left) — the stakeholder does not know the answer and finds the issue complex — and ends at the **Conviction Corner** (top-right) — one answer, 100% complete, that the stakeholder is convinced by.

Two intermediate corners define the four routes:

| Corner | Location | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Complexity Corner | bottom-left | Starting point: complex, no answer yet |
| Clarity Corner | top-left | A range of possible options has been envisaged |
| Certainty Corner | bottom-right | A recommendation grounded in data |
| Conviction Corner | top-right | One answer, fully approved |

---

## The Four Routes to Completion

### 1. Staircase of Expert Execution

The expert already knows the answer before starting. They identify component tasks, assemble a workplan, and execute methodically toward an already-identified outcome.

**Use when:** Someone can credibly claim to have solved the same problem before and knows the way.  
**Do not use when:** The problem is brand-new or unprecedented. The Staircase is neither good nor credible for issues that have never been faced before.

### 2. Submarine of Analytical Research

Unknowns are turned into facts through extensive research and analysis before any answer emerges. The Submarine shoots for the **Certainty Corner** — also called the deductive logic approach.

**Use when:** Lots of reliable data is available, typically rooted in the past or present; the problem has already occurred (inquiry, review, investigation).  
**Do not use when:** The issue is future-oriented and hard facts are unavailable.

Key principle: *The future cannot be analysed; it can only be created.* _source: ch06 §Submarine of Analytical Research_

### 3. Helicopter of Creative Discovery

The Helicopter quickly imposes structure on chaos and generates three or four creative options early — within approximately 5% of available time — reaching the **Clarity Corner** fast, then gradually refining toward the final answer.

**Use when:** No expert answer is available, data is sparse, but stakeholders primarily need creative options and are comfortable with subjective selection.  
**Do not use alone when:** Stakeholders up the decision chain will demand objective data to approve the recommendation. In that case, the Rollercoaster is required.

**Rule 1: 5% Structuring Rule.** Always invest about 5% of the overall time available to arrive quickly at a structure with options before diving deeper. _source: ch06 §Helicopter of Creative Discovery_

**Rule 2: Three-to-Four Options.** Generate three or four creative options when going vertical at the start of a Helicopter or Rollercoaster approach. _source: ch06 §Helicopter of Creative Discovery_

Reaching Clarity early and sharing initial options gives stakeholders time to mull things over, making final agreement easier. When stakeholders have already been thinking about options and arrive at similar conclusions independently, consensus on the recommended answer comes more naturally. _source: ch06 §Helicopter of Creative Discovery_

### 4. Rollercoaster of Strategic Thinking

The optimal route for truly strategic problems. It sequences the other approaches deliberately:

1. **Up (Helicopter phase):** Go vertical to Clarity quickly — generate 3–4 options using creative tools, using only ~5% of available time.
2. **Down (Submarine phase):** Dive into data to eliminate weak options — use whatever data exists to validate or kill ideas, not to generate them.
3. **Push:** Package the surviving solution to win stakeholder Conviction.

**Rule 3: Rollercoaster for Strategic.** Use the Rollercoaster when: (1) no clear answer exists at the beginning, (2) very little data is available throughout, and (3) stakeholders insist on data at the end. _source: ch06 §Rollercoaster of Strategic Thinking_

## The Up–Down–Push Rhythm

The three sections of the Rollercoaster map directly onto the book's three-phase toolset:

| Phase | Goal | What It Produces |
|---|---|---|
| **Up** — Generate Great Ideas Quickly | Reach Clarity Corner fast | Clarity on any strategic issue |
| **Down** — Eliminate Options in No Time | Move from Clarity to Certainty | Certainty that the chosen option is best |
| **Push** — Get the Best Solution Approved | Move from Certainty to Conviction | Stakeholder Conviction |

Up tools: Pyramid Principle, Happy Line, Mutation Game.  
Down tools: Payoff Profiles Matrix, Landscape Analysis, Lean Startup.  
Push tools: Impactful Words, Simple Numbers, Compelling Story.

Each Up technique is compared in the book to a camel in the desert — taking you far on very little water (data). Strategic thinking does not require complete information to generate real ideas. _source: ch04 §Up description_

## The Creative-Then-Analytical Sequencing Principle

The single most important sequencing rule in strategic thinking:

> **To be strategic, first be creative, then be analytical — not the other way around.**

Strategic thinking deals with the future where quality data is scarce. Starting with analysis would be a mistake. Creativity generates options; analysis validates or eliminates them. _source: ch06 §Rollercoaster of Strategic Thinking_

This principle has a corollary: keep your analytical powder dry. The dataset needed to test an idea is much smaller than the dataset needed to generate one; sparse strategic data is best spent on elimination, not discovery. _source: ch06 §HOW TO ELIMINATE OPTIONS IN NO TIME ('DOWN')_

The conclusion chapter reinforces this: *Strategic = Creative + Analytical*. True strategic thinking requires both capabilities applied in sequence — creative comes first, analytical comes second. Most individuals lack one of the two, which is why becoming more strategic is at least as much a personal self-development job as a professional one. _source: ch21 §4_

## What Makes a Problem Truly Strategic?

**Rule 4: Is-It-Strategic Test.** Use the Rollercoaster (not the Staircase or Submarine) when all of the following are true:

1. The problem is **big** in scope.
2. It **lies in the future**.
3. It has **never been done before**.
4. There will be **very little reliable data**.
5. The best answer is **not just a matter of taste**.
6. **Proof will be required** to convince multiple stakeholders.

If a past precedent or expert already has the answer, use the Staircase — it is more efficient. If ample historical data exists, the Submarine may suffice. If stakeholder approval depends primarily on creative taste, the Helicopter alone may be enough. _source: ch06 §IN SUMMARY_

## Mindset vs. Toolset

The Rollercoaster is not just a tool — it is a mindset shift. The book's conclusion reports that participants consistently identify acquiring a radically different mindset — not accumulating more tools — as the true turning point in becoming strategic. _source: ch21 §2_

The 'penny drops' moment is recognizing that everyday analytical routes do not work for big, future problems. Once that realization lands, the Rollercoaster provides the practical mechanism for working smarter, not harder. _source: ch04 §Think description_

Strategy should be treated as the **output** of strategic thinking, not as an object of obsession. Focusing on the method produces a better strategy than fixating on the strategy itself. _source: ch21 opening paragraphs_

## When to Use / When NOT to Use

**Use the Rollercoaster when:**
- The problem is big, future-oriented, unprecedented, and data-scarce.
- Multiple stakeholders will need data-backed proof before approving.
- No credible expert can claim a prior, known answer.
- The issue fails the Staircase and Submarine tests above.

**Do NOT use the Rollercoaster when:**
- An expert genuinely knows the answer already → use the Staircase.
- Ample past/present data exists and the issue has already occurred → use the Submarine.
- Stakeholders are comfortable selecting a preferred option on creative grounds alone → the Helicopter may suffice.
- Time is so constrained that even 5% structuring time is unavailable → revisit scope before choosing any route.

**Rule 5: Think About How You Think.** The first step toward becoming more strategic is to recognize your current problem-solving habits and preferences. Awareness of one's default route enables deliberate choice of the appropriate route for each problem. _source: ch06 opening paragraphs_

## Code Examples

The four routes and their diagnostic criteria can be summarized as a decision matrix. This reflects the book's own IN SUMMARY table from ch06:

```
ROUTE SELECTION DECISION MATRIX
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Route            | Use when                          | Do NOT use when
─────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────
Staircase        | Expert knows answer before        | Problem is new / unprecedented
(Expert)         | starting; same problem solved     |
                 | before                            |
─────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────
Submarine        | Lots of reliable past/present     | Issue is future-oriented;
(Analytical)     | data; problem has already         | hard facts unavailable
                 | occurred (inquiry, review)        |
─────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────
Helicopter       | No expert answer; data sparse;    | Decision chain will demand
(Creative)       | stakeholders OK with subjective   | objective data proof
                 | selection                         |
─────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────
Rollercoaster    | Big + future + never done before  | Answer already known by an
(Strategic)      | + little data + NOT just taste    | expert → Staircase is faster
                 | + proof required for stakeholders |
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
```

The Rollercoaster three-phase sequence:

```
COMPLEXITY CORNER
      │
      │  UP: ~5% of time — generate 3–4 options creatively
      │      Tools: Pyramid Principle, Happy Line, Mutation Game
      ▼
CLARITY CORNER
      │
      │  DOWN: eliminate options using real data
      │        Tools: Payoff Profiles, Landscape Analysis, Lean Startup
      ▼
CERTAINTY CORNER
      │
      │  PUSH: package surviving solution for approval
      │        Tools: Impactful Words, Simple Numbers, Compelling Story
      ▼
CONVICTION CORNER
```
